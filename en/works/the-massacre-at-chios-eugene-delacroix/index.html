What it shows

On 12 April 1822, during the Greek War of Independence, Ottoman troops landed on the island of Chios and ravaged it for months: some twenty thousand inhabitants were killed and almost all the survivors were deported into slavery. Delacroix paints no battle and no hero, only the civilians awaiting their fate; the title under which it was shown says as much: Scenes from the Massacres at Chios; Greek families awaiting death or slavery.

Thirteen figures are arranged in two pyramids. On the left, wounded men, one of them close to death, and women and children clinging to each other, topped by a man in a red fez. On the right, an Ottoman horseman on a rearing horse drags off a woman tied to its back; below, an old woman raises her eyes to the sky and a baby seeks comfort from its dead mother. Between the two groups a young couple embrace, and behind them stretches a landscape of sacked, smoking villages.

Did you know?

  • It was shown at the Paris Salon of 1824, which opened on 25 August, as exhibit no. 450, in the same room as Ingres’s The Vow of Louis XIII. The state bought it that year for 6,000 francs for the Musée du Luxembourg, and in 1874 it was transferred to the Louvre.
  • Antoine-Jean Gros, whose Plague of Jaffa it borrowed from, called it “the massacre of painting”, and Ingres saw in it the “fever and epilepsy” of modern art. Alexandre Dumas wrote that there was always a group of people in front of the picture.
  • Some art historians believe Delacroix reworked the background after seeing three paintings by John Constable at a Paris dealer’s, among them The Hay Wain. The 2019–2020 restoration found almost no later overpainting.
