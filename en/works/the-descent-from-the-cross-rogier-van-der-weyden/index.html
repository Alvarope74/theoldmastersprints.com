The Descent from the Cross by Rogier van der Weyden: meaning, size and where to see it

The Descent from the Cross is an oil on panel by Rogier van der Weyden, painted around 1435. It measures 204.5 × 261.5 cm and is held at the Museo del Prado, Madrid.

What it shows

The dead Christ is lowered from the cross. A young man on the ladder, holding the nails, lets the body down, while Joseph of Arimathea, in a rich robe of gold brocade, and Nicodemus, dressed in red, support it, wrapped in a white linen cloth. In front, the Virgin faints and falls in the same pose as her son; she is held by Saint John, in red, and one of the holy women. On the right, Mary Magdalene wrings her hands, bent double with weeping. On the ground, by the Virgin's hand, lie a skull and a bone: those of Adam, according to tradition.

The ten figures are packed into a shallow space in front of a plain gold background, inside a kind of shrine with tracery in the corners. The picture thus imitates an altarpiece of painted sculptures, which at the time cost more than a painted one. Christ's arched body recalls the shape of a crossbow, a reference to the guild that commissioned it, and the parallel between the Virgin and her son expresses the idea of Mary's compassion, suffering with him. The painted tears on the faces are among the most famous in Netherlandish painting.

Did you know?

  • It was commissioned by the Crossbowmen's Guild of Leuven for its chapel of Our Lady Outside the Walls. In their honour, Van der Weyden painted tiny crossbows into the tracery in the corners.
  • Mary of Hungary, governor of the Netherlands and sister of Charles V, obtained it in exchange for an organ and a copy painted by Michiel Coxcie. By 1549 it was in her palace at Binche, where the future Philip II of Spain saw it; he later took it to the Escorial. According to a story more legend than fact, the ship carrying it to Spain sank and the painting survived thanks to its packing.
  • During the Spanish Civil War it was moved from the Escorial to the Prado, then to Valencia and in 1939 to Geneva with other masterpieces from the museum. It returned the same year and is now in the Prado on deposit from Spain's Patrimonio Nacional. The panel is made of eleven planks of Baltic oak.
