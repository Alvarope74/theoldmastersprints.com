Barge Haulers on the Volga by Ilya Repin: meaning, location and key facts

Barge Haulers on the Volga is an oil on canvas by Ilya Repin, painted between 1870 and 1873. It measures 131.5 × 281 cm and is held at the Russian Museum, Saint Petersburg.

What it shows

Eleven men, harnessed in leather straps, drag a barge along the sandy bank of the Volga under a blazing sun. They move in file, bent double and almost in rags, and the long format of the canvas makes the group seem to advance slowly towards us. In the lead is Kanin, a defrocked priest with a calm expression; at the centre a young man in a pink shirt, Larka, leans back and pulls at his strap.

Repin chose these men as a cross-section of working Russia: among them are a former soldier, a sailor and a painter, and each has his own face and his own way of bearing the effort. In the distance a small steamboat appears, which can be read as a sign that this work's days were numbered, and an inverted Russian flag flies on the barge. The painting is social protest, but also a portrait of the strength of those who endure.

Did you know?

  • Repin made his studies in the summer of 1870 at Shiryaevo on the Volga, where he met several of the haulers in the painting; he painted Kanin on 19 July that year.
  • It was commissioned by Grand Duke Vladimir Alexandrovich, who bought it for 3,000 roubles and hung it in the billiard room of his palace. It reached the Russian Museum in 1918.
  • It won a bronze medal at the 1873 Vienna World Exhibition, and Dostoevsky praised the fact that not one of the haulers shouts from the canvas about how unfortunate he is.

Where to see the original
