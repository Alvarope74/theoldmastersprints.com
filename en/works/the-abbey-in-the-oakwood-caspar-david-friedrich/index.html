The Abbey in the Oakwood by Caspar David Friedrich: meaning, size and where to see it

The Abbey in the Oakwood is an oil on canvas by Caspar David Friedrich, painted between 1809 and 1810. It measures 110.4 × 171 cm and is held at the Alte Nationalgalerie, Berlin.

What it shows

A monastery graveyard in winter, at dusk. In the centre rises the ruin of a Gothic church, with the remains of its window tracery, among leafless oaks. A procession of monks, some of them bearing a coffin, passes a newly dug grave and heads for the portal, where a cross stands. Two candles light the way. The lower third lies in darkness; only the top of the ruin and the tips of the oaks stand out against a glowing sky with a waxing crescent moon.

Friedrich drew on the ruins of Eldena Abbey, near his home town of Greifswald, but this is an imaginary view composed in the studio from sketches made outdoors. The oaks go back to studies made mostly in Neubrandenburg in 1809. The composition is almost symmetrical: the ruin stands on the axis, with four oaks on each side. The painting is about death, and its luminous sky is usually read as the promise of eternal life.

Did you know?

  • It was first shown at the Berlin Academy exhibition of 1810 together with The Monk by the Sea and, at Friedrich’s request, was hung beneath it. The catalogue simply called them “two landscapes in oil”.
  • King Frederick William III of Prussia bought both pictures for 450 thalers, reportedly at the wish of the crown prince, who was fifteen. Goethe had seen this one in Friedrich’s studio on 18 September 1810.
  • Infrared reflectography has revealed a very detailed underdrawing beneath the darkened varnish: a youthful Christ on the cross in the portal and, inside the church, an altar with burning candles. The painting was restored and went back on view in 2016.
