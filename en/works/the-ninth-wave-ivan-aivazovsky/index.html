The Ninth Wave by Ivan Aivazovsky: meaning, location and key facts

The Ninth Wave is an oil on canvas by Ivan Aivazovsky, painted in 1850. It measures 221 × 332 cm and is held at the Russian Museum, Saint Petersburg.

What it shows

Dawn after a night of storm. A small group of shipwrecked sailors clings to a broken mast, all that remains of their ship, and one of them waves a red cloth. Behind them rises a huge wave of translucent green, while the sun breaks through the haze and turns the sky and water yellow, pink and violet.

The painting shows both the violence and the beauty of nature. The warm tones soften the menace of the sea and leave the survivors a hope of rescue, and some see in the cross-shaped wreckage a Christian metaphor of salvation. Aivazovsky, who usually painted from memory without preliminary sketches, fills the large canvas almost entirely with colour and light.

Did you know?

  • The title refers to an old seafaring belief that the ninth wave of a storm was the most destructive.
  • Lewis Carroll saw it at the Hermitage on his 1867 trip to Russia and described it in his diary as perhaps the most striking of all Russian paintings. It moved to the Russian Museum in 1897.
  • Aivazovsky signed and dated the work on the mast itself: “Айвазовскій 1850”.

Where to see the original

It hangs in the Russian Museum in Saint Petersburg, the city's great museum of Russian art, housed in the Mikhailovsky Palace. It measures 2.21 × 3.32 metres.
