The Three Philosophers by Giorgione: meaning, size and where to see it

The Three Philosophers is an oil on canvas by Giorgione, painted between about 1508 and 1509. It measures 125.5 × 146.2 cm and is held at the Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna.

What it shows

Three men of different ages have gathered at the edge of a wood, beside the dark mouth of a cave. The youngest, seated on the left, holds a set square and compasses and stares at the cave as if measuring it. Beside him stands a middle-aged man in a turban and red robe, and next to him an old man with a white beard and yellow cloak, holding a sheet of astronomical figures. Behind them a valley opens out, with a village and distant mountains. Giorgione finished it around 1508–1509, shortly before his death, probably for the Venetian nobleman Taddeo Contarini, who had an interest in alchemy and the occult.

No one knows for sure who they are. Until the late 19th century they were taken to be the Three Magi before the grotto of Bethlehem; today they are read as the three ages of man, as the passing of knowledge from Greek philosophy to the Arab scholars and from them to the Renaissance, or as an allusion to Plato’s allegory of the cave, among many other proposals. That ambiguity, together with the warm light and the landscape that envelops the figures, is typical of Giorgione, one of the most mysterious painters in European art.

Did you know?

  • The title comes from Marcantonio Michiel, who saw it around 1525 in Taddeo Contarini’s house in Venice and noted that it had been finished by Sebastiano del Piombo; a recent infrared reflectogram has found no evidence of his hand.
  • The sheet held by the old man bears the word “eclipsis” beside an astronomical diagram. The historian Augusto Gentili linked it to the great planetary conjunction and the eclipse of 1503, then believed to herald the coming of the Antichrist.
  • The canvas was cut down on the left: the dark rock once took up more than half of the picture. In the 17th century it belonged to Archduke Leopold Wilhelm of Austria, and David Teniers the Younger reproduced it in the Theatrum Pictorium, the illustrated catalogue of his collection.
