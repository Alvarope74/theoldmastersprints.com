The Hay Wain by John Constable: meaning, size and where to see it

The Hay Wain is an oil on canvas by John Constable, painted in 1821. It measures 130.2 × 185.4 cm and is held at the National Gallery, London.

What it shows

Three horses pull an empty wooden wain that has stopped in the ford of the River Stour on a summer afternoon. On the left, among the trees, stands the cottage of Willy Lott, a neighbouring farmer; on the bank a dog watches the wain. In the distance, across the river, a sunlit meadow opens out where haymakers are at work, and a great sky of white and grey clouds stretches over the whole landscape.

The scene is beside Flatford Mill, which belonged to Constable’s father; since the Stour forms the border between two counties, the left bank is in Suffolk and the right bank in Essex. Constable painted it as one of his “six-footers”, the large canvases about six feet wide that he prepared for the Royal Academy’s annual exhibitions. The wain is thought to have stopped in the river to let the horses drink and to make the wooden wheels swell and grip their iron tyres, which the summer heat loosened.

Did you know?

  • It was shown at the Royal Academy in 1821 under the title Landscape: Noon and failed to find a buyer.
  • It caused a sensation at the Paris Salon of 1824: Charles X awarded it a gold medal, a cast of which is set into the frame, and it inspired French painters such as Delacroix. Stendhal wrote that they had never seen anything like it and that its truthfulness was what struck them most.
  • Constable painted a full-scale oil sketch, made with a palette knife, which is now in the Victoria and Albert Museum. In a 2005 poll by BBC Radio 4’s Today programme it was voted the second most popular painting in a British gallery, behind only Turner’s Fighting Temeraire.

Where to see the original

It is in the National Gallery in London, to which the collector Henry Vaughan gave it in 1886. It measures 130.2 × 185.4 cm. Willy Lott’s Cottage still stands almost unchanged beside Flatford Mill, although none of the trees in the painting exist today.
