Baroque art for your home: how to pair Vermeer, Rembrandt and Velázquez with a modern living room

Baroque is the painting of the 17th century: dark backgrounds, directed light and scenes that seem frozen in time. It can look solemn, but well chosen it is exactly what a minimalist living room needs: a piece with depth that warms up white walls.

Vermeer: quiet light for kitchens and dining rooms

Vermeer’s interiors are small and vertical. The Milkmaid (c. 1658–1660), with its bread and jug, seems made for a kitchen or dining room. Girl with a Pearl Earring stands out alone on a narrow wall, and View of Delft, wide and full of sky, works above a chest of drawers.

Rembrandt: drama for a big wall

The Night Watch is over 3.5 metres tall at the Rijksmuseum; as a landscape A1 print it keeps that cinematic energy. The Jewish Bride is more intimate: warm reds and golds for a bedroom.

Velázquez: the painting that looks back

Las Meninas is almost square and full of figures looking out at you. In a hallway or entrance it feels like someone is welcoming you in. It works best large, in A2 or A1.

For something lighter: Rococo

In the 18th century the Baroque grows lighter: pastel colours, gardens and humour. Fragonard’s The Swing (1767), all pink and green, is the perfect example for a bedroom or dressing room.

Frames and walls

Baroque’s dark backgrounds stand out strongly on white, pale grey or sage-green walls. A thin black frame makes it feel modern; a gilt frame keeps it classic. Avoid hanging several very dark Baroque paintings together: a single one, well lit, has more impact.

Frequently asked questions

Does a Baroque painting work in a modern living room?
Yes. Its dark backgrounds and directed light add depth to a minimalist room; with a thin black frame it blends in very well.
Which Vermeer painting suits a kitchen?
The Milkmaid, with its domestic scene of bread and milk, is the classic choice for a kitchen or dining room.
What is the difference between Baroque and Rococo?
Baroque (17th century) is dramatic, with strong contrasts of light and shadow; Rococo (18th century) is lighter, with pastel colours and gentler subjects.
