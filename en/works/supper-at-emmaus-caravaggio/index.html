Supper at Emmaus by Caravaggio: meaning, size and where to see it

Supper at Emmaus is an oil on canvas by Caravaggio, painted in 1601. It measures 196.2 × 141 cm and is held at the National Gallery, London.

What it shows

This is the episode from the Gospel of Luke (24:30–31) in which the risen Jesus, unrecognised, sits down to supper with two disciples at Emmaus and reveals himself as he blesses the bread, just before vanishing from their sight. At the centre, Christ, young and beardless, raises his hand in blessing. On the right, Cleopas, with a pilgrim’s scallop shell on his chest, flings his arms wide, while on the left the other disciple, his sleeve torn at the elbow, grips his chair as he starts to rise. The standing innkeeper, his face in shadow, notices nothing.

The figures are life-sized and the background is dark and empty, so the light falling from the left picks them out as if on a stage. On the tablecloth lies a still life of bread, roast chicken, wine and a basket of fruit teetering on the edge of the table, as if about to fall towards the viewer. Cleopas’s outstretched arms and the overhanging basket break the picture plane and draw the viewer into the scene.

Did you know?

  • It was commissioned and paid for by Ciriaco Mattei, brother of Cardinal Girolamo Mattei, in Rome in 1601.
  • Christ is shown beardless, unlike in other works by the painter such as The Calling of Saint Matthew; this may reflect the Gospel of Mark (16:12), which says he appeared to the disciples “in another form”.
  • Caravaggio painted a second version of the subject in 1606, now in the Pinacoteca di Brera in Milan, with far more restrained gestures; he made it after fleeing Rome following the death of Ranuccio Tomassoni.
