Ophelia by John Everett Millais: meaning, location and key facts

Ophelia is an oil on canvas by John Everett Millais, painted between 1851 and 1852. It measures 111.8 × 76.2 cm and is held at Tate Britain, London.

What it shows

Ophelia, from Shakespeare’s Hamlet, floats singing in a river before she drowns, arms open and lips parted. She is surrounded by flowers and plants painted with botanical precision, many with symbolic meaning: poppies for death, violets for faithfulness.

John Everett Millais, a member of the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, painted the landscape from life beside the Hogsmill River in Surrey over months in 1851. He added the figure later in his London studio.

Did you know?

  • The model, Elizabeth Siddal, posed in a bathtub full of water; when the lamps warming it went out she fell ill, and her father demanded that Millais pay the doctor’s bills.
  • Henry Tate gave it to the Tate in 1894.
  • It is now on display at Tate Britain in London.

Where to see the original
