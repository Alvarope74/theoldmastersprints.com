The Last Day of Pompeii by Karl Bryullov: meaning, size and where to see it

The Last Day of Pompeii is an oil on canvas by Karl Bryullov, painted between 1830 and 1833. It measures 466 × 651 cm and is held at the Russian Museum, Saint Petersburg.

What it shows

It is the night of the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79. On the Street of Tombs, on the outskirts of Pompeii, the inhabitants flee in terror as ash falls and statues topple from their pedestals. The foreground is made of separate groups: two sons carry their old father on their shoulders; a young man tries to lift his fallen mother, who begs him to flee without her, as Pliny the Younger's mother did; a woman clasps her two daughters; a bolting horse and a broken chariot lead the eye into the distance. On the left, on the steps of the tomb of Scaurus, Bryullov painted himself as a Pompeian artist escaping with his box of brushes balanced on his head.

Bryullov visited the ruins of Pompeii in 1827 and was so struck by the remains of the Street of Tombs that he decided to set the scene there, reproducing the authentic paving and kerb stones. He drew on the letters of Pliny the Younger to Tacitus, which describe the eruption, and on models such as Raphael's The School of Athens. Countess Yuliya Samoylova and her adopted daughters are thought to have sat for several of the female figures. The painting stands between Neoclassicism, in its classically modelled bodies, and Romanticism, in its drama, its chiaroscuro and its subject: the power of nature over man.

Did you know?

  • It was commissioned by Count Anatoly Demidov, whom Bryullov met in Naples, for 40,000 francs, to be finished by 1830. The painter took so long that Demidov threatened to cancel the commission: by the end of 1830 Bryullov had only outlined the figures in two colours, and he completed it in 1833.
  • It was a European sensation. In Rome it was shown in Bryullov's studio on Via San Claudio; in Milan the painter received a standing ovation in a theatre, and at the Paris Salon of 1834 it won a gold medal. Walter Scott is said to have studied it for an hour and declared it not an ordinary painting but an epic. Edward Bulwer-Lytton saw it in Milan and in 1834 published his novel The Last Days of Pompeii.
  • In 1834 Demidov presented it to Tsar Nicholas I, who displayed it in the Winter Palace and in 1836 gave it to the Imperial Academy of Arts. In 1851 it became the centrepiece of the Russian painting display at the New Hermitage, and in 1897 it passed to the Russian Museum. Gogol wrote that its paints "burn and hit you in the eye".

Where to see the original

It is in the Russian Museum in Saint Petersburg, the city's great museum of Russian art, housed in the Mikhailovsky Palace. It measures about 4.6 × 6.5 metres.
