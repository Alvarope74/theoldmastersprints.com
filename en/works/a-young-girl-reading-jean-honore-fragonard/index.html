What it shows

A young woman whom no one has been able to identify sits reading in profile, her back very straight despite the large violet cushion she leans on, her left elbow on the armrest. She holds a small book in her right hand and does not look up: the whole scene is her concentration. Fragonard painted it around 1770, when he was one of the most celebrated Rococo painters in Paris, and it is one of his quietest works: less a portrait than a scene of everyday life.

The lemon-yellow dress, with its white ruff and cuffs and violet ribbons, and the girl’s rosy skin glow against a dark background, framed by the violet cushion and the brown armrest. Fragonard painted fast, with loose, energetic brushstrokes plainly visible in the frills of the dress, and uses them to set apart the textures of wall, fabric and wood. It belongs to his series of so-called “fantasy figures”, young sitters painted quickly and freely.

Did you know?

  • X-rays have shown that underneath there is another head, which looked out towards the viewer; Fragonard painted over it to create the version we see today.
  • It belonged to the French surgeon Théodore Tuffier and reached the United States before 1930, in the New York collection of Alfred W. Erickson, founder of the advertising agency McCann Erickson.
  • The National Gallery of Art bought it in 1961, after the death of Erickson’s widow, with funds given by Ailsa Mellon Bruce, daughter of the collector Andrew W. Mellon.
