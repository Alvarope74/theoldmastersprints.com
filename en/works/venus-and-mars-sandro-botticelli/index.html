Venus and Mars by Sandro Botticelli: meaning, size and where to see it

Venus and Mars is a tempera and oil on poplar panel by Sandro Botticelli, painted around 1485. It measures 69.2 × 173.4 cm and is held at the National Gallery, London.

What it shows

Venus and Mars recline facing each other on the grass in a small grove. Venus, awake and dressed in a white gown with gold borders, calmly watches Mars, who is fast asleep, almost naked, with his head thrown back. Four baby satyrs play around them: two carry off his helmet and lance, another has crawled into his breastplate under his arm, and the fourth blows a conch shell in his ear without managing to wake him. At top right, a swarm of wasps comes out of a hollow in a tree trunk and hovers by the god's head.

The picture shows love conquering war: the goddess of beauty has disarmed the god of war, who sleeps defenceless. Its long format and the close view of the figures suggest it was made to be set into a piece of furniture or the wooden panelling of a bedroom, probably to celebrate a wedding. The satyrs playing with the weapons come from Lucian's description of a lost ancient painting, the wedding of Alexander the Great and Roxana, and the pose of the gods recalls Lucretius's lines on Mars surrendering to Venus. The wasps may refer to the Vespucci, Botticelli's neighbours and patrons, whose name means "little wasps" in Italian.

Did you know?

  • It is the only one of Botticelli's large mythological paintings not in the Uffizi in Florence. The English collector Alexander Barker bought it in Florence, and the National Gallery acquired it at the sale of his collection at Christie's in 1874, for £1,050.
  • In 2010 the art historian David Bellingham identified the fruit held by the satyr at lower right as a thorn apple (Datura), a plant that causes drowsiness; the National Gallery accepted the identification in 2017.
  • The models for Venus and Mars have often been identified as Simonetta Vespucci and Giuliano de' Medici, but both had been dead for years when it was painted: Simonetta died in 1476 and Giuliano was murdered in the Pazzi conspiracy in 1478.
