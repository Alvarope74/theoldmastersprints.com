The Swing by Jean-Honoré Fragonard: meaning, size and where to see it

The Swing is an oil on canvas by Jean-Honoré Fragonard, painted in 1767. It measures 81 × 64.2 cm and is held at the Wallace Collection, London.

What it shows

In a lush garden that is almost a forest, a young woman in a full pink silk dress flies high on a swing. As she rises she kicks one of her slippers into the air, sending it towards a statue of Cupid who holds a finger to his lips for silence. Below on the left, half hidden in the rose bushes, a young man gazes up at her from a privileged angle, while on the right, in the shadows, an older man pushes the swing, unaware of anything.

It is a scene of flirtation and mischief, the defining image of French Rococo: light brushwork, pastel colours and a light that gathers on the young woman amid the green. Fragonard turns a garden pastime into a small amorous comedy in which every character, even the statues, has a part to play.

Did you know?

  • According to the memoirs of the playwright Charles Collé, the commission was first turned down by the history painter Gabriel-François Doyen, who passed it to Fragonard. The client, a gentleman of the court, wanted his mistress shown on a swing pushed by a bishop.
  • Its full title is The Happy Accidents of the Swing (Les Hasards heureux de l'escarpolette).
  • The statue of Cupid asking for silence is based on Menacing Love, a sculpture by Étienne-Maurice Falconet.

Where to see the original

It hangs in the Wallace Collection in London, a free museum in Hertford House on Manchester Square that holds one of the finest collections of 18th-century French painting. It measures 81 × 64.2 cm.
